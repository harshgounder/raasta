## 1_what_actually_covers_hm_02_and_what_does_not

The target is not merely a drug-interaction chatbot. It is a chain of distinct obligations: preserve prescription text; interpret a schedule; resolve an Indian product; split its ingredients and strengths; accumulate exposure across products; apply patient-specific rules; rank alerts; and explain the result without claiming unsupported safety. A failure at identity resolution invalidates every downstream calculation.

**Case study: a catalogue that looks like a safety engine.** The Indian Medicine MCP server is unusually relevant because it implements exact search, fuzzy search, composition search, composition analysis, manufacturer filtering, and alternative suggestions. Its documented implementation uses Python, FastMCP, and JSON; its composition analysis extracts ingredient and dosage text rather than demonstrating a patient-specific dosing engine. [1_what_actually_covers_hm_02_and_what_does_not[0]] [2]

That distinction changes the build plan. A polished wrapper could make this server appear to answer the whole problem, while still knowing nothing dependable about a particular child's weight or a patient's renal dosing restrictions. The recommended product therefore exposes separate status chips: **Identity confirmed**, **Schedule confirmed**, **Dose rule available**, and **Interaction coverage available**. These are proposed interface states, not existing capabilities of the server.

**Case study: a broad safety assistant that deliberately stops short.** ModelScope's medsafe-copilot describes drug-drug queries for **2-10 names**, food-drug conflicts, plain-language label explanations, and older-person medication-list screening. Its MVP uses synthetic examples, focuses on **200+ common drug aliases**, and does not provide dose-adjustment advice. [1_what_actually_covers_hm_02_and_what_does_not[1]] [21]

Its value is architectural rather than clinical completeness: a compact SQLite-backed core and Gradio surface can demonstrate explanations and screening without a large local model. The implication is not that Chinese alias coverage solves India; it is that a small, understandable safety interaction can already be assembled. **Decision:** compose a resolver, a reviewed knowledge table, and an explanatory interface; do not confuse any one of them with full prescription validation.

## 10_what_not_to_build_and_why

**Do not use an LLM's fluent answer as the safety database.** Lohita15's repository describes hosted Llama-based interaction analysis, while the MCP project's documented functions focus on catalogue/composition operations. Neither description by itself establishes a validated patient-specific dosing reference. Their detailed audits are the relevant evidence; the recommended separation is deterministic clinical findings first, optional language generation second.

**Do not mistake an API specification for a finished app.** The cuurai candidate is explicitly described in its audit as a specification-oriented project. It can supply contracts and concepts, but treating it as an already-running safety engine understates the build and data work.

**Do not train a new molecular interaction predictor for this event.** DeepPurpose is a research toolkit whose advertised task family includes DDI, not a packaged Indian-brand reconciliation and dose-checking product. Its audit records what is actually available. Even a successful predictor installation does not resolve Indian brand identity, formulation, schedule ambiguity, or pharmacist approval.

**Do not fork a full EHR just to obtain a medicine form.** OpenEMR is a broad EHR/practice-management repository and its displayed licence is GPL-3.0. Its audit below records the actual stack and scope. A 16-hour interface-focused team would inherit substantial unrelated surface area; distributing a derivative also requires understanding the actual licence obligations.

**Do not copy Gitee DrugManagement as though a public page grants permission.** Its host explicitly reports no declared LICENSE file. The showcased pharmacist and compatibility workflows remain useful competitive evidence, but an explicit grant is needed before treating the code as reusable. Confidence: High. **Do not use the ModelScope synthetic knowledge as real clinical evidence.** Its own description identifies synthetic samples, limited alias coverage, and no dose-adjustment advice. Keep the structural idea; replace the knowledge with permitted, reviewed sources. Confidence: High for the stated limitation. [10_what_not_to_build_and_why[1]] [21]

**Do not scrape a commercial checker because its website is free to visit.** DrugBank commercial/API leads were found, but a verified public price, free quota, India coverage commitment, and redistribution right were not established. Pricing is **CANNOT VERIFY**; a current provider pricing page or written trial/usage grant would settle it. A public checker is not a licence to republish its database.

**Do not make OCR, handwriting, speech, or a large local model the critical path.** Typed input is the stated requirement. PaddleOCR is a relevant optional component, but model choice and measured laptop memory remain separate decisions in its audit. Screenshots and handwriting introduce an identity-confirmation problem before any safety logic can run.

**Do not show "safe" when a check was not performed.** Unknown product, uncertain strength, missing schedule maximum, unavailable rule, and missing renal context are different states. The proposed interface should name each state and let the pharmacist or user resolve it rather than compressing uncertainty into a reassuring score.

**Decision:** exclude features that increase apparent certainty faster than they increase verified coverage. This is the central product-risk filter for the shortlist.

The non-obvious tension is that the most impressive-looking systems can be the least dependable hackathon foundations. A multi-agent cloud architecture offers breadth but adds credentials and deployment; a research model offers novelty but does not identify Indian combination products; a public drug website offers useful answers but not necessarily reusable data. Conversely, a small reviewed SQLite/JSON subset can be operationally modest and visually persuasive.

The cross-language comparison reinforces that distinction. The Indian MCP server contributes product/composition operations; the Chinese ModelScope example contributes compact screening and explanation structure; Gitee DrugManagement contributes a richer pharmacist workflow but an explicit licence obstacle. They differ across **mechanism, clinical scope, legal reusability, and implementation burden**, not just language. [synthesis[0]] [2][synthesis[1]] [21][synthesis[2]] [1]

**Final choice:** attempt Path 1 briefly, keep Path 2 ready, and build the ingredient X-ray around a pharmacist-approved fixture pack. Make the central promise narrow: **"We show what these verified products contain, how the confirmed schedule accumulates exposure, which reviewed rules apply, and what we cannot assess."** Do not promise universal safety, autonomous prescribing, or nationwide catalogue completeness.

The strongest 30-second result is therefore not "our AI knows medicines." It is a visible chain from an Indian brand name to an ingredient, from two products to a hidden shared exposure, and from a patient context to a reviewable finding. That is both more understandable to a non-technical judge and better aligned with the problem's actual traps.
