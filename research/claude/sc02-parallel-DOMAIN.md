### Standards: distinguish a kerb from a kerb ramp

| Design element | MoHUA Harmonised Guidelines 2021 | IRC:103-2022 and version caveat |
|---|---|---|
| Clear walking width | Minimum **1,200 mm** walkway; **1,800 mm** for two-way movement. [5] | Recommends a minimum **2 m clear walking zone**, allowing two wheelchairs to pass. [27] |
| Kerb height | Kerb-ramp guidance discusses kerbs up to **200 mm**, with higher kerbs creating slope difficulties. This is **not a climbable step allowance**. [5] | Footpath kerb height **150 mm**. [27] |
| Kerb-ramp gradient | Not steeper than **1:12**. [5] | Not steeper than **1:15**. The older **IRC:103-2012** used **1:12**. [27][29] |

**Takeaway:** A footpath can exist and still be unusable. Your system should assess **clear width, transitions, surface, gradient and continuity**, with each requirement tied to the relevant standard version.

A ratio of **1:12** means one unit of rise over twelve horizontal units, approximately **8.33%**; **1:15** is approximately **6.67%**. These are arithmetic conversions, not evidence that every user can independently negotiate either slope.

A **23 February 2026** correction is important: progressive compliance under existing Rule 15(1) standards and Accessible India targets must **continue unabated**, while the mandatory, non-negotiable baseline is developed. **Decision:** Say, **"We help people navigate today's gaps and help authorities identify tomorrow's repairs."** Never imply that an alternative route excuses an inaccessible public street.

## 8. Ten "What If" Questions Judges Will Ask

1. **What if a vehicle parks there after the photograph?**  
   We cannot know without a newer observation. The proposed system shows timestamps, accepts reports and expires evidence. We will not call it "real-time" unless we actually operate and measure a live update process.

2. **What if the AI misses a pole, hole or broken ramp?**  
   That is a safety-relevant false negative. Low-confidence or obscured sections remain unknown; critical transitions need checking. A high average detection score is not permission to label every route safe.

3. **What if no accessible route exists?**  
   Say **"No verified route found"**, identify the blocking link and offer a reporting or assistance option. Do not secretly substitute a route with steps or a dangerous road detour.

4. **What if GPS puts the user on the wrong side of the road?**  
   Side-of-street location and crossing confirmation are required. If positioning is ambiguous, the proposed interface should ask the user to confirm a landmark or starting side instead of issuing confident crossing instructions.

5. **What if it rains or a street becomes waterlogged?**  
   Old dry-weather imagery cannot certify current passability. Wet-surface evidence and Jothady's case justify caution, but not an invented flood-depth threshold. The proposal should flag uncertainty and avoid obscured critical segments. [41][30]

6. **What if a route works for a powered wheelchair but not a manual chair, pram or crutch user?**  
   Accessibility is profile-dependent. Ask about equipment and preferences; show width, slope and surface evidence. Never infer someone's climbing ability from the fact that they use a wheelchair.

7. **What if the user cannot see the screen or has no reliable internet?**  
   The proposed website needs screen-reader support, keyboard access, high contrast, large controls and accessible instructions. Cached route information can help offline, but its age must remain visible; offline does not mean current. It must not replace a cane or mobility training.

8. **What if Google Maps already does this?**  
   Google already documents wheelchair-accessible walking routes globally where data exists. Our proposed contribution is local evidence about continuous clearance, surface condition and freshness. We must demonstrate that contribution on Jaipur routes, not merely assert it. [14]

9. **What if collecting images violates privacy or becomes too expensive?**  
   Start with a small corridor and permitted image collection. Minimise retention, blur identifiable faces/plates, and check image-use terms. Image access, update cost and staffing are unresolved operational questions, not free resources to assume in the pitch.

10. **What if someone is injured after following the route? Can you guarantee safety?**  
    No. State the limits plainly, avoid safety guarantees and do not rely on a disclaimer instead of careful design. Begin with supervised testing and accessible feedback, then expand only when dangerous errors and update performance are understood. A claim that the system reduces injuries remains unproven.

## 9. Synthesis

The central tension is between **accessibility as a property of a place** and **accessibility as a continuous, time-dependent journey**. Wheelmap and AccessNow help describe places; Sugamya Bharat supports redress; Evelity provides adapted wayfinding; Soundscape's original service offered spatial awareness; Google documents accessible walking routes. These functions overlap but are not interchangeable. [9][32][33][10][19][14]

| Approach | Mechanism and scope | Time horizon | Main trade-off |
|---|---|---|---|
| Standards, enforcement and physical repair | Change the environment and establish obligations. [11] | Durable infrastructure and compliance | A route app cannot substitute for them. |
| Place information and complaints | Describe destinations or report barriers. [9][32][33] | Contribution and resolution dependent | Useful information may not certify the connecting journey. |
| Routing and wayfinding | Guide movement using available route/site information. [14][10][19] | Depends on coverage and data currency | Guidance is not proof that every current obstacle is known. |
| Proposed image-plus-community system | Attach user-specific barrier evidence and freshness to street segments | Initially a limited, checked pilot | Wider coverage increases the burden of keeping evidence current. |

**Takeaway:** The strongest system combines these layers instead of pretending one replaces the others.

Karan Shah's bollards reveal a conflict between vehicle exclusion and wheelchair access. The Karnataka audit reveals a conflict between protective infrastructure and sensory guidance. Prakash Jothady's injury reveals a conflict between yesterday's route and today's surface. Together, they argue against a single binary label called "accessible." [22][24][30]

**Final recommendation:** Promise **a transparent route recommendation with evidence, personalisation and honest uncertainty**. Prove one useful Jaipur journey, expose its remaining barriers, and use that evidence to support physical change. That is a more credible and more humane pitch than promising that AI makes every street safe.
